# 00 — Product Vision: SLM Distillation from Local Frontier Teachers

*Status: THESIS doc — the north star. Everything else in this folder is engineering in service of this.*
*Author: OGLab / CharlieD.  Started: 2026-04-18.*

---

## One-line version

> **Distill task-specific Small Language Models (SLMs) by running the world's largest LLMs as teachers on commodity hardware, using symbolic chain-of-thought traces filtered and hardened by a swarm of adversarial agents.**

The AeroLLM runtime, the MLX streaming work, and the batching research in the rest of this folder are not the product. They are the *economic foundation* that makes the product possible — the thing that lets a single consumer box produce enough high-quality teacher tokens per day to train a useful SLM without renting a B200 cluster.

---

## Why this matters

The distillation playbook is no longer speculative. Microsoft's Phi series, Orca 2, and Orca-AgentInstruct have publicly shown that synthetic data generated by a frontier model — if it's carefully structured and filtered — produces student models that punch massively above their parameter weight. The research literature (SCoTD, Lion, GAD, Mentor-KD, Distribution-Aligned Sequence Distillation) has moved from "does distillation work?" to "what's the best way to generate and filter the teacher corpus?"

The bottleneck has shifted from *algorithm* to *access*. If you want to distill from GPT-4-class models, you pay OpenAI per token and live with their rate limits, ToS, and opacity. If you want to distill from an open frontier model (DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2, Llama 3.1 405B, GLM-4.6), you need hardware that costs more than a house — unless you're willing to trade latency for throughput and let a layer-streamed inference engine grind for hours.

OGLab's bet is that *the latency trade is exactly right for distillation workloads*. A chatbot has to answer in seconds. A distillation corpus generator has to answer in a week. Batching 500 prompts through a layer-streamed 671B model on a 2 TB laptop is a terrible chat experience and an excellent training-pair factory.

## The inference engine: AeroLLM

The inference layer of this pipeline — Stage 2 below — ships as **AeroLLM** (short: *AeroLLM*).

- **Auto** — the always-on autoresearch loop that sweeps knobs, measures, and lands improvements under `config/tuning.yml` / `config/tuning-mlx.yml` without human handholding. See `docs/tuning-loop.md`.
- **Air** — credit to the [AeroLLM](https://github.com/cdarnell/aerollm) project (Gavin Li et al.). Their layer-streaming pattern on CUDA is the approach we're porting to Apple Silicon and extending with batching + an autoresearch wrapper.

**Track priority — MLX first.** As of April 2026, no public project ships an AeroLLM equivalent for MLX. CUDA is a solved space (AeroLLM, KTransformers, vLLM, llama.cpp all have working solutions), so our CUDA track is a upstream checkout of upstream AeroLLM plus an application-layer batching scheduler — we carry it for parity and to serve NVIDIA users, not to invent anything. The MLX track is where the engineering risk and the novel research live; it's what the autoresearch loop is optimizing; it's what makes or breaks the economic argument for the distillation product on Apple hardware.

**Naming hedge.** If the MLX streaming design diverges materially from AeroLLM's CUDA approach (different eviction model, different expert cache, different scheduler shape), we'll rename the MLX track to something more specific to Apple-Silicon SSD-streamed inference. "AeroLLM" is the working name while the architectures remain close relatives.

## Why OGLab's default work item is AeroLLM

OGLab is a research lab, and its superpower is the autoresearch loop — the always-on process that sweeps knobs, measures, learns, and lands improvements without human handholding. But **autoresearch is only as interesting as the models it has access to.** An autoresearch loop hyper-tuning a 7B model is useful engineering; an autoresearch loop hyper-tuning a *frontier* model — a model most people can't load on any hardware they own, and have to rent GPUs or pay an API to use — is structurally different. The lab is doing something no one else is positioned to do.

That's the dependency. The rest of OGLab's program — the SLM distillation product, the adversarial swarm, the measurement framework, the publishing cadence — only matters if the lab *has its own frontier teacher to study*. Otherwise it's tuning against someone else's API, on someone else's rate limits, under someone else's terms of service, with someone else's pricing model driving the experimental design. That's not a research lab; that's a customer.

**And — equally important — it's a customer who's handed over their data sovereignty.** Every seed prompt sent to an API reveals the lab's domain of interest, its prompt-engineering choices, its experimental design, and the resulting CoT corpus itself. For a lab whose core IP *is* the prompt distribution and the derived training data, routing through a commercial API means the provider sees the work before the students do. Some providers explicitly reserve the right to use API traffic to improve their own models; others reserve the right to change that policy later. Either way, your corpus stops being yours the moment it crosses the network boundary.

**AeroLLM is what breaks both dependencies — operational and informational.** If it works, OGLab operates on a model most researchers can never run locally — DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2, Llama 405B, GLM-4.6 — and every prompt, rationale, and answer stays on the lab's own hardware. If it doesn't work, the lab reverts to paying for access like everyone else, hands over the data anyway, and the autoresearch loop spends its cleverness on the 7B tier.

So the default work item — the thing the autoresearch loop is pointed at, the thing that gets engineering priority, the thing we're willing to grind on for months before anything else in the roadmap moves — is **getting AeroLLM to produce one valid token on a frontier model on a 24 GB box.** Everything downstream (batching scale, swarm design, corpus size, student quality) is hypothetical until that `status=ok` row lands.

First: get it to work. Then: tune it. Then: build on top of it. Order matters.

## Problem statement

A small team building task-specific SLMs today faces three bad options:

1. **Pay a frontier API** (OpenAI / Anthropic / Google). Fast and high quality, but expensive at scale, rate-limited, ToS-restricted for distillation in some cases, and leaks your prompts and distribution to the provider.
2. **Rent GPU hours** (H100 / H200 / B200). High-quality open-model teacher, but $2–12 per GPU-hour, and you need 4–8 of them to fit DeepSeek-V3 in VRAM. A week-long distillation run is real money.
3. **Run a tiny open model locally** (Llama 3 8B, Mistral 7B). Cheap, private, but the teacher's ceiling is the student's ceiling — you can't distill knowledge the teacher doesn't have.

What's missing: **a way to run a *true frontier* open model as a teacher on hardware you already own, at throughputs that are slow but economically viable for batch corpus generation.**

That's the AeroLLM + batching thesis, restated for a specific customer: the solo ML researcher / small team building a vertical SLM.

---

## Goal (the product, stated plainly)

Build a local distillation pipeline that can:

1. Host a frontier open-weights LLM (DeepSeek-V3 671B, Kimi K2 1T, Llama 3.1 405B, GLM-4.6 750B) on a single consumer machine via layer-streamed inference.
2. Batch hundreds of prompts through that teacher per pass so the per-token economics work out.
3. Elicit *symbolic* chain-of-thought traces — not just final answers — so the student learns the reasoning, not the response surface.
4. Filter and harden those traces with an adversarial agent swarm: critic agents score, red-team agents probe, judge agents adjudicate, and only traces that survive get written to the training corpus.
5. Output a training corpus that's immediately usable by standard SLM fine-tuning stacks (HF TRL, Unsloth, MLX-LM fine-tune).

Everything else in the roadmap is in service of those five steps.

---

## Non-goals

Things this product is explicitly **not** trying to do, and won't be judged on:

- **Beating frontier inference latency.** We're not competing with vLLM on chat tokens/sec. Our latency is intentionally bad; our *throughput-per-watt-per-dollar* for offline corpus generation is what matters.
- **Training a new frontier model from scratch.** We use frontier models as teachers. We don't pretrain.
- **Serving the distilled SLM at scale.** Once trained, the SLM is handed off — the user deploys it however they want.
- **Replacing commercial APIs for latency-sensitive production workloads.** If you need sub-second responses, pay OpenAI. We serve a different workload.
- **Supporting every frontier model day-one.** Per-family streaming forward passes (DeepSeek, Llama, Kimi, GLM) each take engineering. We land them one at a time.

## The pipeline (product architecture, end-to-end)

```
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STAGE 1 — Seed prompt generation                                         │
│  ─ Domain-scoped prompt templates (math, code, SQL, legal, medical, …)    │
│  ─ Diversity expansion via self-instruct / Orca-AgentInstruct-style flows │
│  ─ Output: a queue of ~10k–1M seed prompts per run                        │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STAGE 2 — Frontier teacher inference  ← AEROLLM LIVES HERE               │
│  ─ Layer-streamed forward pass                                            │
│      • MLX (primary track)  — novel; no prior art on Apple Silicon        │
│      • CUDA (reference track) — the AeroLLM runtime + batching scheduler      │
│  ─ Static-batch offline scheduler: N prompts through each layer pass      │
│  ─ Symbolic CoT elicitation (structured scratchpad, not free-form text)   │
│  ─ Output: (prompt, rationale, answer) triples                            │
│                                                                           │
│  This is the bottleneck. Every knob in config/tuning*.yml exists to       │
│  make this stage's throughput-per-watt-per-dollar go up.                  │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STAGE 3 — Adversarial agent swarm (filter + harden)                      │
│  ─ Critic: is the rationale logically valid? step-by-step check           │
│  ─ Red-team: can you find a prompt variant where this reasoning breaks?   │
│  ─ Judge: given critic + red-team reports, keep / revise / discard        │
│  ─ Optional: Lion / GAD-style referee loop to tighten hard examples       │
│  ─ Output: hardened (prompt, rationale, answer) triples + rejection log   │
│                                                                           │
│  Runs on a smaller fast model (e.g., Qwen3-8B-4bit on MLX) in parallel    │
│  with Stage 2 — the frontier teacher is the expensive hot lane, the swarm │
│  is the cheap cold lane consuming its output.                             │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STAGE 4 — Corpus curation                                                │
│  ─ Deduplication (MinHash / embedding-based)                              │
│  ─ Distribution balancing (SCoTD-style, covering reasoning styles)        │
│  ─ Difficulty curriculum ordering (easy → hard for staged training)       │
│  ─ Output: sharded JSONL ready for SFT / DPO / GRPO                       │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                   │
                                   ▼
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  STAGE 5 — SLM training (offboard, out of scope for this repo)            │
│  ─ Hand off the corpus to Unsloth / TRL / MLX-LM fine-tune                │
│  ─ Evaluate on held-out task benchmarks                                   │
│  ─ Iterate: feed eval failures back to Stage 1 as new seed prompts        │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The loop from Stage 5 back to Stage 1 is the thing that makes this a *product* rather than a one-shot: the SLM's failures become the next batch of teacher queries, which become the next batch of training examples.

## Why symbolic chain-of-thought specifically

Plain answer-distillation (teach the student what the teacher said) leaves most of the teacher's capability on the floor. What makes the Orca / Phi / SCoTD approach work is that the student is trained on the *reasoning process*, not just the output — and a *symbolic* CoT (where intermediate steps are explicit variables, formulas, or named subgoals) transfers better than free-form natural-language CoT because it's less verbose, less style-coupled, and more verifiable by the adversarial swarm.

Reference points:
- **SCoTD** (Li et al., 2306.14050) — symbolic CoT distillation from very-large teachers into commonsense-reasoning students, 75 %+ of teacher perf at 3 % of params.
- **Orca 2** (Mitra et al., 2311.11045) — "cautious reasoning" traces; student learns *when* to use which reasoning strategy.
- **Phi-4 technical report** (Abdin et al., 2412.08905) — curriculum of synthetic data, quality matters more than quantity.
- **Mentor-KD** (Kang et al., 2410.09037) — mentor model generates missing CoT supervision for weak teachers.

---

## Why an adversarial swarm

Synthetic data generated by a single pass of a single model has known failure modes: confabulated citations, subtly wrong reasoning that *sounds* right, distribution collapse toward the teacher's stylistic tics. A swarm addresses all three:

- **Critic agents** catch logical errors the teacher made. A critic pass on CoT traces routinely catches 10–30 % rationale errors even in frontier-model output. [Lightman et al., 2305.20050 — "Let's Verify Step by Step"]
- **Red-team agents** generate adversarial prompt variants that break shallow memorization. This is the GAD / Lion insight — if the student can be distilled to ace only the easy prompts, it won't generalize. [Lion: 2305.12870; GAD: 2511.10643]
- **Judge agents** adjudicate disagreements and assign confidence scores — so the curation stage can weight examples by quality, not just keep-or-toss.

Practically: Stages 2 and 3 run as producer-consumer. The frontier teacher (expensive, high-latency, batched at 50–500 prompts) produces candidate traces into a queue. The swarm (cheap, fast, running on Qwen3-8B or similar) drains the queue in parallel. Teacher throughput is the ceiling; the swarm just has to keep up, which it does easily because it's running a 100× smaller model.

---

## Why THIS runtime, THIS hardware

The whole thesis collapses if frontier teacher inference costs $10k/month to run. AeroLLM + layer streaming + batching is the argument that **you can run a 671B-class teacher on a $4k laptop and produce enough training pairs per day to be economically interesting**.

Back-of-envelope, using the verdagon.dev numbers as anchors (70B model, 16 GB consumer machine, 4.85 s/token at batch 500):

- Target model: 671B MoE, ~37B active. Per-token compute is ~halfway between the 70B number and a 405B dense number; estimated 30–60 s/token at batch 500 on a 24 GB Apple M5 with SSD streaming. **This is a guess — the whole measurement framework in this folder exists to replace it with a real number.**
- At 45 s/token × batch 500 = 500 tokens every 45 s = ~11 tokens/sec aggregate across the batch.
- Assume 500 output tokens per training example (prompt + CoT + answer) = ~1 example every 2 s aggregate.
- 24-hour run = ~40k training examples per day per machine.

40k high-quality CoT training examples per day is a non-trivial corpus in 2–4 weeks. The Phi-4 recipe shows that a few hundred thousand carefully curated examples can produce frontier-competitive task-specific students. The math works if the batching throughput numbers hold up — which is exactly what the measurement plan in `04-measurement-log.md` is designed to establish.

## Success metrics

Stated in priority order, so we don't fool ourselves.

1. **M1 — Frontier teacher runs at all.** At least one of {DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2, Llama 3.1 405B, GLM-4.6} produces a `status=ok` BenchRun with `tokens_out>0` on a 24 GB M5. This is Gate 3 from `docs/mlx-streaming-plan.md`. Binary pass/fail. Without this, nothing else matters.
2. **M2 — Batched teacher throughput is measured.** We know the tokens/sec/watt curve at batch sizes {1, 10, 50, 100, 500} on the target hardware. This replaces the "guess" above with numbers.
3. **M3 — End-to-end pipeline produces one valid training example.** Seed → teacher → swarm → corpus, all the way through, for a single real prompt, with swarm rejection logs showing the filter worked.
4. **M4 — 1k-example corpus generated overnight.** First real distillation run. Proves throughput is sustainable.
5. **M5 — Trained SLM beats untrained baseline on held-out task.** The distillation actually works — the whole product delivers on its promise. This is the "is the business real?" metric.
6. **M6 — 100k-example corpus / 1M-example corpus.** Scale milestones. Each order of magnitude is a stop point to look at cost curves, quality drift, and iteration speed.

Failure modes that should kill or pivot the project:
- Frontier teacher tokens/sec/watt never gets within 10× of cloud-GPU economics even after batching + autoresearch tuning.
- Swarm-filtered corpus quality is indistinguishable from unfiltered — the adversarial step adds cost but no value.
- Trained SLM doesn't transfer the frontier teacher's capabilities (student ceiling is way below teacher).

## Roadmap (Now / Next / Later)

**Now (Q2 2026) — MLX-first.**
- Land MLX streaming for one frontier family (DeepSeek-V3 first — see `docs/mlx-streaming-plan.md`). This is gate 1. Nothing else on the MLX track starts until the model loads.
- Build the static-batch offline scheduler as an application layer on top of MLX inference (works regardless of whether streaming is ready yet — can be proven on gpt-oss-20B first).
- First end-to-end dry run on MLX: one seed prompt → AeroLLM teacher → swarm → one training example.
- Reproduce the verdagon.dev batching curve on MLX with a non-streaming model (gpt-oss-20B) as a validation of the batching math before we apply it to streamed frontier models.

**Now — CUDA reference track (lower priority, kept alive).**
- AeroLLM CUDA backend + application-layer batching scheduler, same scheduler code the MLX track uses.
- Measure the verdagon.dev curve on CUDA for a sanity-check baseline.
- Not blocking on the distillation pipeline — MLX is the hot path.

**Next (Q3 2026).**
- Adversarial swarm v1: critic + judge agents using Qwen3-8B-4bit on MLX fast path.
- Symbolic CoT elicitation templates (math, code, SQL domains — pick one to start).
- First 1k-example corpus + first distillation run on a 1.5B-3B student.
- Publish the measurement data (hardware-indexed tokens/sec-per-watt for batched teachers).

**Later (Q4 2026+).**
- Red-team agent with iterative refinement (Lion/GAD-style loop).
- Multi-teacher ensembling (route easy prompts to smaller teachers, hard prompts to frontier).
- Curriculum-aware corpus ordering.
- First public vertical SLM demo — a specific task where our distilled student beats a commercial 8B baseline.

## Risks & assumptions

Named, so they can be tested rather than pretended away.

- **Assumption: layer-streamed batching works at N > 50.** The verdagon.dev data shows it works at N=500 on a 70B. We haven't measured it on a 671B MoE. If batching saturates at N=20 because KV cache fills memory, the economics tighten dramatically.
- **Assumption: frontier open models are actually frontier-capable on our target tasks.** DeepSeek-V3 / Kimi K2 / Llama 405B are competitive on benchmarks, but the student's ceiling is the teacher's ceiling. If the teacher's weak on domain X, we can't distill to a strong X-student. Mitigation: pick domains where open frontier is known to be competitive.
- **Assumption: adversarial swarm filtering adds signal.** Plausibly, for some domains, a simple accept/reject classifier is enough and the full swarm is overkill. The first 1k-example run will tell us whether the swarm's rejection logs correlate with downstream student performance.
- **Risk: AeroLLM's native batching story is worse than it sounds.** Our current belief is that layer streaming is single-prompt-per-layer in the reference implementation, which would make batching a one-line forward-loop patch. If it's instead a deeper rewrite, timelines slip. Measuring this is open-question #2 in `README.md`.
- **Risk: terms-of-service on open frontier models restrict distillation output.** Most open-weights licenses (DeepSeek, Llama, Kimi) explicitly permit distillation; GLM's license is the one to re-check. Worst case, the resulting SLM's license inherits restrictions — which may or may not matter depending on the use case.
