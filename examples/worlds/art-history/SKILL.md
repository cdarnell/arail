### Dance

- **Ancient Ritual & Choral Dance** (`dance-ancient-ritual`) — Communal ritual and choral dance in ancient Greece, including the singing-dancing chorus at the heart of tragedy.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Classical Russian Ballet** (`dance-ballet-classical-russian`) — The grand classical ballet perfected at the Imperial Russian theaters by Marius Petipa, codifying the full-evening, multi-act ballet spectacle.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Ballet d'Action Reform** (`dance-ballet-daction-reform`) — The Enlightenment reform that made ballet a self-sufficient dramatic narrative told through expressive movement rather than ornament and convention.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Ballet de Cour** (`dance-ballet-de-cour`) — French court ballet under Louis XIV, where Beauchamp's codification of the five positions turned aristocratic spectacle into a formal technique.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Romantic Ballet** (`dance-ballet-romantic`) — The early-nineteenth-century ballet of ethereal sylphs and ghosts, dancing on pointe in white tulle, that made the ballerina the art's center.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Ballets Russes** (`dance-ballets-russes`) — Diaghilev's itinerant company that detonated ballet into modernism, fusing radical choreography, music, and design into avant-garde theater.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Contemporary & Street Dance** (`dance-contemporary-and-street`) — The plural landscape of today's theatrical dance and the global rise of street and hip-hop dance from urban vernacular roots.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Jazz & Tap Dance** (`dance-jazz-tap-dance`) — American vernacular dance with African-American roots — rhythmic, syncopated jazz dance and the percussive footwork of tap.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Medieval Folk & Court Dance** (`dance-medieval-folk`) — Medieval communal and court dancing — the sung circle-dance carole, instrumental estampie, and the imagery of the danse macabre.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Modern Dance** (`dance-modern-dance`) — The early-twentieth-century revolt against ballet's formality, inventing a new expressive vocabulary rooted in the dancer's own body and feeling.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Postmodern Dance** (`dance-postmodern-dance`) — The mid-century avant-garde that stripped dance of narrative and hierarchy, embracing chance, everyday movement, and pure form.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica
- **Renaissance Court Dance (Balletto)** (`dance-renaissance-court`) — Italian Renaissance court dance, codified by professional dancing masters whose treatises gave Europe its first detailed choreographic record.
  - Source: The Oxford Dictionary of Dance; International Encyclopedia of Dance; Encyclopædia Britannica

### Eras & Movements

- **Classical Antiquity** (`era-antiquity`) — The Greco-Roman foundation (~800 BCE–500 CE) that bequeathed proportion, mimesis, and the canon of genres to every later art.
  - Source: Gombrich, The Story of Art (Greek & Roman art); The Met Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History; Encyclopædia Britannica (Classical antiquity)
- **The Baroque** (`era-baroque`) — ~1600–1750: drama, motion, and grandeur — chiaroscuro, tonality, the birth of opera, and court ballet.
  - Source: Janson's History of Art (Baroque); Taruskin, Oxford History of Western Music (tonality & opera); Encyclopædia Britannica (Baroque)
- **Enlightenment & the Classical Style** (`era-enlightenment-classical`) — ~1750–1820: reason, clarity, and balance — Rococo's last sweetness giving way to Neoclassical order and the Classical style.
  - Source: Gombrich, The Story of Art (Age of Reason); Taruskin, Oxford History of Western Music (Classical style); Encyclopædia Britannica (Enlightenment; Neoclassicism)
- **The Middle Ages** (`era-medieval`) — Roughly 500–1400: a Christianised, symbolic art of chant, icon, illuminated manuscript, and Gothic verticality.
  - Source: Janson's History of Art (Medieval art); Taruskin, Oxford History of Western Music (chant & early polyphony); Encyclopædia Britannica (Middle Ages)
- **Modernism** (`era-modernism`) — ~1860–1945: the break with tradition — abstraction, atonality, stream of consciousness, and the birth of cinema.
  - Source: Gombrich, The Story of Art (Modernism); Bordwell & Thompson, Film History (early cinema); Encyclopædia Britannica (Modernism)
- **Postmodern & Contemporary** (`era-postmodern-contemporary`) — 1945–present: pluralism, pastiche, mass media, and the dissolving line between high and popular art.
  - Source: Janson's History of Art (Postwar & Contemporary); Bordwell & Thompson, Film History (postwar cinema); Encyclopædia Britannica (Postmodernism)
- **The Renaissance** (`era-renaissance`) — ~1400–1600: the rebirth of classical learning, linear perspective, humanism, and the individual artist.
  - Source: Gombrich, The Story of Art (Renaissance); Grove Art Online (Renaissance); Encyclopædia Britannica (Renaissance)
- **Romanticism & the 19th Century** (`era-romanticism`) — ~1800–1900: emotion, nature, the sublime, and the individual genius — plus Realism's counter-turn to ordinary life.
  - Source: Janson's History of Art (Romanticism & Realism); Taruskin, Oxford History of Western Music (Romantic era); Encyclopædia Britannica (Romanticism)

### Film & Cinema

- **Auteur Theory** (`film-auteur-theory`) — The critical idea that the director is the true author of a film, stamping even studio work with a personal vision.
  - Source: Andrew Sarris, Notes on the Auteur Theory; François Truffaut, A Certain Tendency of the French Cinema (Cahiers du cinéma); Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction
- **The Blockbuster Era** (`film-blockbuster`) — The mid-1970s shift to wide-release, heavily marketed event films built for mass simultaneous audiences and franchises.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; Tom Shone, Blockbuster; British Film Institute (BFI)
- **Citizen Kane** (`film-citizen-kane`) — Orson Welles's 1941 debut, a landmark that fused deep focus and fractured chronology into a new expressive maturity for the medium.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; British Film Institute (BFI) Sight and Sound; The Oxford History of World Cinema
- **The Digital & Streaming Era** (`film-digital-streaming`) — The recent shift to digital capture, computer-generated imagery, and on-demand streaming distribution that reshaped how films are made and seen.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; British Film Institute (BFI); The Oxford History of World Cinema
- **The Documentary Tradition** (`film-documentary`) — The nonfiction lineage of cinema, from staged actuality to observational movements claiming a fresh access to reality.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; Bill Nichols, Introduction to Documentary; The Oxford History of World Cinema
- **French New Wave** (`film-french-new-wave`) — A late-1950s–1960s French movement of young critic-directors who made personal, formally playful films breaking classical rules.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; Cahiers du cinéma; The Oxford History of World Cinema
- **German Expressionism** (`film-german-expressionism`) — A 1920s German movement that externalized psychological states through distorted sets, sharp shadow, and stylized design.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; Lotte Eisner, The Haunted Screen; British Film Institute (BFI)
- **Classical Hollywood & the Studio System** (`film-hollywood-studio`) — The vertically integrated American film industry that, from the late 1920s, mass-produced genre pictures and manufactured movie stars.
  - Source: Bordwell, Staiger & Thompson, The Classical Hollywood Cinema; Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; British Film Institute (BFI)
- **Italian Neorealism** (`film-italian-neorealism`) — A post-WWII Italian movement that filmed ordinary lives on real streets with non-professional actors and minimal artifice.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; André Bazin, What Is Cinema?; The Oxford History of World Cinema
- **New Hollywood** (`film-new-hollywood`) — The late-1960s–1970s American renaissance in which a generation of director-driven filmmakers revitalized Hollywood with personal, formally daring films.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; Peter Biskind, Easy Riders, Raging Bulls; British Film Institute (BFI)
- **The Silent Era** (`film-silent-era`) — The first decades of cinema (~1895–1929) in which the medium invented its own visual grammar before synchronized sound.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; British Film Institute (BFI); The Oxford History of World Cinema
- **Soviet Montage** (`film-soviet-montage`) — A 1920s Soviet movement that argued meaning in film is generated by the collision of edited shots, not by individual images.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; Sergei Eisenstein, Film Form; The Oxford History of World Cinema
- **World Cinema** (`film-world-cinema`) — The global tradition of major national art cinemas whose masters expanded film's formal and cultural range beyond Hollywood.
  - Source: Bordwell & Thompson, Film History: An Introduction; The Oxford History of World Cinema; British Film Institute (BFI) Sight and Sound

### Music

- **Baroque Tonality & Basso Continuo** (`music-baroque-tonality`) — The Baroque era replaced modes with functional major-minor tonality, anchored by basso continuo and dramatized by the concerto.
  - Source: The Grove Dictionary of Music and Musicians (Oxford); Richard Taruskin, The Oxford History of Western Music, vol. 2; Encyclopædia Britannica, 'concerto'
- **Ludwig van Beethoven** (`music-beethoven`) — The composer who bridged Classical and Romantic, expanding form and turning music into personal expression.

- **The Classical Style & Sonata Form** (`music-classical-style`) — The mid-18th-century turn to clear melody, balanced phrasing, and the dramatic architecture of sonata form.
  - Source: The Grove Dictionary of Music and Musicians (Oxford); Charles Rosen, The Classical Style; Encyclopædia Britannica, 'sonata form'
- **Gregorian Chant** (`music-gregorian-chant`) — The unaccompanied monophonic liturgical chant of the Western church and the cradle of music notation.

- **Impressionist Music** (`music-impressionist-music`) — A turn-of-the-century French style prizing timbre, color, and atmosphere over traditional development.

- **Medieval Polyphony** (`music-medieval-polyphony`) — The medieval invention of multiple simultaneous voices, from early organum to the rhythmic sophistication of ars nova.

- **The Birth of Opera** (`music-opera-birth`) — Opera emerged around 1600 from attempts to revive Greek drama through music, with Monteverdi giving it its first masterpiece.

- **Renaissance Polyphony** (`music-renaissance-polyphony`) — The golden age of imitative vocal counterpoint, perfected by Josquin and codified by Palestrina.

- **Rock, Pop & Hip-Hop** (`music-rock-and-pop`) — The recorded popular era, in which rock, pop, and hip-hop became the dominant music of the late 20th and early 21st centuries.
